# Short-form scripts

Turns a topic into a shootable 30–45s script for Reels / TikTok / Shorts.
Method distilled from 14 high-performing 2026 creator breakdowns (see `references/sources.md`).

**Core premise: scripting cannot fix a bad idea.** Validate the idea first.
Retention is the only metric that matters — on Shorts, the gap between 100K and 10M views
is roughly a **5% difference in average watch time**.


## The spine

Every short is built on the same four beats. Only the value delivery changes.

```
HOOK  →  OPEN LOOP  →  VALUE DELIVERY  →  PAYOFF
0–3s      3–8s          8–35s              last beat
```

The hook stops the scroll. The **open loop bridges from the hook into the body** and buys the
next 20 seconds. The value delivery is the reason the video exists, and **its shape is decided
by the content type, not by a storytelling framework**. The payoff closes the loops.


## Step 1 — Validate the idea (never skip)

Confirm at least one:

- **Trend validation** — 3–4 of the top creators in the niche posted this topic recently.
- **Wow test** — it made *you* say "wow" (≈90% odds the audience agrees).
- **Script bending** — a *proven structure* from a viral video in an adjacent niche, bent to this topic. Structure only, never the words.
- **Deeper problem** — the emotional struggle under the technical one.

Passes none of these? Say so, and offer three bent alternatives before scripting.

## Step 2 — Classify the content type

Pick exactly one from `references/content-types.md`. This decides the middle of the script:

| Type | Value delivery |
|---|---|
| **New model / new tool** | What's actually new → 2–3 standout capabilities → what it beats or replaces |
| **News story** | Key facts → brief context → why it matters to them |
| **Tutorial / process** | The steps in order → the gotcha most people hit |
| **Opinion / take** | The claim → the evidence → the pre-empted objection |
| **Comparison** | The contenders → 2–3 axes → the verdict |

**State the type in the output.** If a story spans two, pick the one matching *why the viewer
clicked* and let the other type's material live in the payoff. If nothing fits, say so and
propose an outline rather than forcing it.

## Step 3 — Hook (0–3s)

Full type list, templates and proven lines in `references/hook-library.md`. That file also maps
which hook types and lines fit which content type from Step 2 ("Which hooks fit which content
type") — use it to shortlist candidates before drafting the 3 variants, rather than picking a
hook cold.

1. **Stop, then Stack.** Interrupt the scroll, *then* stack tension — contrast, a challenged belief, or stakes.
2. **Layer three hooks** — spoken + on-screen text + visual. Assume mute playback.
3. **Two visual changes inside the first 3 seconds.**
4. **No introduction.** No greeting, no name, no "in today's video".
5. **Specific enough to sting.** Name the exact situation, not a vague one.
6. **Make it something done to the viewer.** A hook describing a change is weaker than a hook describing an action taken against them. "ChatGPT just stopped writing in your favourite author's style" reports; "ChatGPT has **banned you** from writing in your favourite author's style" accuses. Use active verbs with the company as the subject and the viewer as the object — *banned you*, *locked you out of*, *taken away*. Keep it truthful: if the source describes observed behaviour rather than an announced ban, the strong verb belongs only where the facts support it.
7. **Or withhold the subject so the hook opens its own loop.** "ChatGPT has just banned users from running this one workflow" holds back *which* workflow, so the hook does the job of a loop before the loop arrives. The **"this one [thing]"** construction is the workhorse here. It buys you a loop beat that can then raise stakes rather than merely explain.
8. **Put a recency marker in the first line** — *now*, *just*, *as of today*. "You can **now** sell your face to an AI company" tells the viewer this is current; without it the same sentence could be three years old, and the scroll continues. One word, and it is the difference between news and a fact.
9. **"You", not "I".**
10. **Pre-block the obvious answer** on question hooks.

Always offer **3 hook variants**, labelled by type, with a recommendation.

## Step 4 — Open loop (3–8s)

**Its own beat, immediately after the hook.** Separate job from the mid-script loop openers in
Step 6: this one is structural — it converts a stopped scroll into a committed watch by naming
a reward that arrives later.

**It must:**
- **Continue the hook's own thread.** The open loop takes the last idea in the hook and pushes it one step further. A loop that introduces a new subject reads as a hard switch and loses the viewer at the seam — this is the most common way the beat fails.
- **Raise the stakes, not just point at the next fact.** The loop earns the next twenty seconds by making the viewer feel the consequence, so aim it at *them* and at what could happen. Compare: "And the contract is vague about where it ends up" merely flags a document, while "And your face could end up anywhere without you even knowing" puts the viewer inside the outcome. Same information, and only the second one is worth staying for. Point the loop at the worst realistic outcome, in the viewer's own terms.
- **Keep it broad enough to include everyone still watching.** The hook is where you get specific; the loop is where you widen. A loop that assumes a particular habit — "the prompt you've been using has probably already broken" — silently excludes every viewer who doesn't have that prompt, and they leave at exactly the moment you were trying to hold them. Aim at the general consequence instead: "And it's taken away one of the most useful things you could do with it." Specific enough to mean something, broad enough that nobody rules themselves out.
- **Sharpen it with one intensifier.** *Even*, *already*, *still* — a single small word that makes the consequence land harder. "without you knowing" states a fact; "without you **even** knowing" makes it unfair. One per loop, placed on the word carrying the stakes.
- **Be one short line.** Ten to twelve words. If it needs a comma-splice or a two-part construction, it is too long — cut it down until a person could say it in one breath.
- **Withhold, not summarise.** The moment the open loop gives away the answer, the video is over.
- **Point forward to something real** that genuinely lands later in the script. An unpaid open loop is clickbait and reads as one.
- **Land it by 0:08.**

**Patterns that work:**
> "And the third one works in total silence." *(hook named three things — this picks one out)*
> "But the number that matters here is two and a half."
> "And it's the free one that's winning."
> "The reason is buried at the bottom of the announcement."

**Anti-patterns:**
- "Let me explain." · "Here's why." · "Stay tuned." — empty, promise nothing.
- **Two-part reversals**: "But X isn't the story. Y is." Wordy, and the negative half does no work. Say the positive half only.
- **Restating the hook** in different words. "And the share button is the whole reason why" after a hook about the share button gives the viewer nothing new.

**When the script has multiple points** (see Step 5), the hook names the count and the open loop reaches into the list:
> Hook: "Meta's glasses just learned three new tricks." → Loop: "And the third one works in total silence."

**One open loop at this position.** Stacking two is where scripts start to feel like they're stalling.

## Step 5 — Value delivery (8–35s)

Follow the outline for the type you picked in Step 2 — beat by beat, from
`references/content-types.md`. This is the part of the script that earns the watch; treat the
outline as the structure, not as a suggestion.

Write the beats before the words. Target **30–45 seconds total** (~90–120 spoken words).

**One idea per script.** A 35-second video carries a single point. Everything in the value
delivery either sets that point up or pays it off; anything that is merely *also true about the
subject* comes out. Three interesting facts sitting side by side feel like a summary and retain
like one — pick the strongest and cut the other two. If cutting them hurts, they are separate videos.

**The value delivery has to flow out of the open loop.** The first line must answer it, or the
script reads as two videos stapled together.

**Build the flow into the sentence itself.** Pick up the loop's own noun and keep going. Done
properly this needs no linking phrase at all:

> Loop: "And it's the biggest asset manager in the world." → "BlackRock and Meta are putting 14 billion dollars into one campus in El Paso."
> Loop: "And it happened the moment you tapped share." → "Sharing a chat turns it into a public web page."
> Loop: "And the third one works in total silence." → "One. Threads is now in your glasses."

**Explainer lead-ins are the wrong fix.** "You see" is the worst of them — it's a narrator's
phrase, nobody says it in conversation, and it announces that an explanation is coming instead of
just giving it. "Here's the thing", "The truth is" and "What you need to understand is" fail the
same way. **If a line needs one of these to connect, the line is in the wrong order — rewrite it
so the subject of the loop is the subject of the next sentence.**

Through the rest of the body, join beats with ordinary spoken words — "and", "so", "but", "which
means". Each beat should sound caused by the one before it, never merely next in the list. Vary
them; the same join three times running reads as a template.

**When the point genuinely needs more than one item, number them out loud.** One. Two. Three.
The count goes in the hook, the open loop points at one of them, and each number gets a spoken
"One…", "Two…", "Three…" on the line. Three items maximum. Unnumbered multi-point bodies are
where scripts turn to mush — the viewer loses track of how much is left and swipes.

Do not swap in a storytelling framework here by default. The frameworks in
`references/frameworks.md` (HRST, Escalating POV, Retention Dance, Hero's Journey) are
**optional overlays** for when a piece is genuinely narrative — reach for one deliberately,
and say why. The type outline is the default.

## Step 6 — Retention locks inside the body

Drop a **loop opener every 20–30 seconds** — confirms the current value, teases bigger value
next, resets the attention timer:

> "Here's the thing though…" · "But that's not even the most interesting part…" · "This is where it gets crazy…" · "Most people stop here, but actually…"

Apply the six **Story Locks** and sentence-level craft from `references/story-locks.md`:
term branding, embedded truths, thought narration, negative frames, loop openers, contrast words.

Structural rules:
- **South Park rule** — "this happens, **but** this happens, **therefore** this happens." Never "and then".
- **Vary sentence length, and check it by counting.** Write the word count of each spoken line in a row — `9 · 11 · 8 · 16 · 15 · 18 · 15` — and look at it before delivering. **Four consecutive lines within about three words of each other is a fail**, however good each line is on its own; that stretch is where the ear checks out. A working script has at least two punches under six words. Uniform length is monotone, and monotone is churn.
- **Pick one word for a thing and keep using it.** Rotating synonyms across a script — *holes* → *flaws* → *vulnerabilities* — is one of the clearest tells of AI writing, because a person picks a word and sticks with it. Deliberate repetition of a keyword across beats is a chain that holds the argument together, so keep it. Reach for a synonym only when the meaning genuinely changes.
- **At most two lines in a script may open with a conjunction.** "And…" and "So…" are good spoken joins, which is exactly why they multiply — four of seven lines opening on one reads as an outline being expanded rather than a person reasoning. When a line needs a join, first try starting it on its own subject instead: "So attackers get the same list you do" → "Attackers get that list the same second you do."
- **Write whole sentences, never clipped fragments.** Short is good; truncated is not. A sentence keeps its subject and verb even when it's five words long. "First time in two years" → "That's the first time in two years." "Same chips, more model." → "It's the same chips doing more work." Dropped subjects read as written copy, and on camera they sound like a headline being recited.
- **One list of three per script, at most — and only when the three items are doing different jobs.** Rhythmic repetition of the same grammatical shape ("every data centre, every model, every lab") is the single clearest tell of AI writing. Replace it with one concrete example: "the chips that basically every AI lab is buying." If a triple genuinely earns its place, it is the numbered list from Step 5, spoken as one/two/three.
- **Anchor with hard facts** — real numbers, dates, names. One number that carries the story beats five that don't.
- **Simple language.** No jargon in the first 30 seconds; define it on the spot if unavoidable.
- **Keep each value beat to one short, plain sentence.** A beat carrying a subordinate clause is a beat doing two jobs. "The licensing terms are often loose, which leaves the seller guessing about which products they'll appear to endorse" is one clause too many and one register too formal — cut it to "The terms are loose, so people find their face on products they never agreed to." Say it the way you'd say it to a friend; if a word wouldn't survive that test, replace it.
- **Say what a thing does, not what it's called.** Internal model names, version numbers and codenames mean nothing to the viewer and cost a beat to explain. "The AI got noticeably better at understanding what you're looking at" lands; "upgraded to their Muse Spark models" does not. Names belong in the caption.
- **Sub-hooks between points** — every transition reveals that what they expected isn't the whole story.
- **State things positively.** Say what is true, not what isn't. Two constructions to avoid on sight:
  - **Stacked negatives** — "no jailbreak, no workaround, no trick." Replace with the positive fact: "They just did it."
  - **Was-never reversals** — "the problem was never X, it's Y." Replace with the claim alone: "The problem is Y."
- **Every transition must name what it's pointing at.** Abstract nouns pointing backwards make the viewer stop and work out the referent, and that pause is where they swipe. "And the timing tells you why" forces the question *the timing of what?* — name it: "And this started while OpenAI is being sued by book authors." The same trap catches "that dynamic", "this pattern", "the reason for that". If a transition contains a noun the viewer can't instantly resolve to something already said, rewrite it with the thing itself.
- **Cut empty pointer phrases.** "This is the one." · "Here's the thing." · "And this is important." They announce significance instead of delivering it, and they read as stalling. If a beat is the best one, its content proves that.

## Step 7 — Payoff and close

- **Most rewarding or outrageous moment last**, then **cut immediately**. The slow outro is where they swipe.
- **Start the payoff with "So".** The last line is a conclusion drawn from what came before, and "So…" is how a person says that out loud. Without it the line arrives as a fresh statement and reads like a written summary bolted on: "Your face has become something you can sell once" versus "So your face is now something you can sell once, and someone else can keep using." Use *and so*, *which is why* or *so now* when a straight "So" doesn't sit right.
- **Take one of two shapes — definite, or a question to the viewer.** A payoff that hedges dies on delivery.
  - **Definite:** state the outcome flatly, in the future or present tense, with no qualifier attached. "So the plan is you book a taxi and a Chinese car turns up" is limp because *the plan is* holds it at arm's length. "So soon you'll book a taxi in London, and a Chinese car with nobody driving it will pull up" commits.
  - **Question:** put the decision to the viewer in one short line — "So would you get in it?" Ask something the audience genuinely splits on; a rhetorical question with one obvious answer gets no replies.

**The definite statement is the default. Reach for the question only when the story puts the
viewer in a decision** — something they'd personally accept or refuse, like getting into a
driverless car or selling their likeness. Most stories don't, and asking anyway produces limp
engagement bait ("So what do you think about Meta's data centre?") that reads as filler.

**When a question is warranted, commit first and ask second.** The strongest version is one
sentence of outcome followed by a short question:

> "So soon you'll order a taxi in London and a Chinese car with no driver will pull up. Are you getting in it?"

The statement pays off the script; the question hands the argument to the comments. Asking
without committing first throws away the payoff.
- **Qualifiers live in the fact beats, never in the payoff.** If something is a plan rather than a launch, say "plans to" back in the value delivery where it belongs. That earns the right to end on a clean, definite line without overclaiming.
- **Close every loop you opened** — the hook's and the open loop's. Unpaid loops collapse engagement even when watch time holds.
- **The last line has to survive being read literally.** Say it out loud to someone with no context: if they'd ask "what does that mean?", rewrite it. Clever inversions and paradoxes ("the rare tools are the popular ones") feel sharp on the page and land as nonsense in the ear. Prefer a plain sentence that states the consequence, or a concrete instruction the viewer can act on today.
- **Never say "bye", "like and subscribe", or wave.** Instead: summarise, create a need for the next video, or end on a reflection.

## Step 8 — Pressure-test before delivering

Report failures honestly rather than quietly passing:

- [ ] Content type declared, and the value delivery follows that type's outline beat for beat
- [ ] **The script makes one point.** Name it in a single sentence. Anything not serving it is cut
- [ ] Hook lands under 3 seconds, names a specific situation
- [ ] **Hook frames it as something done to the viewer**, or withholds the subject ("this one workflow") so it opens its own loop
- [ ] **Recency marker in the first line** — *now*, *just*, *as of today*
- [ ] Audio + text + visual hook all present
- [ ] **Open loop is one short line by 0:08, continues the hook's thread, and is paid off later**
- [ ] **Open loop names a consequence for the viewer**, not just the next fact in the story
- [ ] **Open loop is broad enough that no one watching rules themselves out** — no assumed habits or setups
- [ ] **Every transition names its referent** — no "the timing", "that dynamic", "this pattern"
- [ ] **The line after the open loop answers it by carrying its subject forward** — the flow is in the sentence, not in a linking phrase
- [ ] **Zero explainer lead-ins** — "You see", "Here's the thing", "The truth is", "What you need to understand is"
- [ ] Joins between beats are ordinary spoken words, and they vary
- [ ] **Multi-point body is numbered out loud** — one, two, three — with the count in the hook
- [ ] Zero hedge words — *if, maybe, might, could, probably* → *when, the reason, once you*
- [ ] **Zero stacked negatives and zero was-never reversals** — every claim stated positively
- [ ] **Zero empty pointer phrases** — "this is the one", "here's the thing", "and this is important"
- [ ] **Zero internal names, codenames or version numbers** in the spoken lines — describe the behaviour instead
- [ ] Zero introduction bloat, zero greeting
- [ ] A mid-script loop opener lands by ~25s
- [ ] Contrast word in the hook and at every transition
- [ ] **Sentence-length map written out and checked** — no four consecutive lines within ~3 words of each other, at least two punches under six words
- [ ] **One word per thing, repeated** — no synonym rotation (*holes → flaws → vulnerabilities*)
- [ ] **Two lines maximum open with a conjunction**
- [ ] **Every sentence is complete** — no dropped subjects, no headline fragments
- [ ] **No rhythmic triples** — "every X, every Y, every Z" and its relatives are gone
- [ ] Every loop opened is closed before the end
- [ ] Body matches the hook's promise
- [ ] **Every value beat is one short, plain sentence** — no stacked subordinate clauses, no formal register
- [ ] **The final line makes literal sense to someone with no context** — no paradoxes, no inversions
- [ ] **The payoff opens with "So"** (or *and so* / *so now* / *which is why*)
- [ ] **The payoff is flatly definite** — zero qualifiers in the final line, with any "plans to" moved back into the value beats
- [ ] **A closing question only if the story puts the viewer in a real decision** — and then it comes *after* the definite statement, never instead of it
- [ ] Ends on the payoff, no outro
- [ ] **The middle is specific to this story** — swap in a different subject and it should break
- [ ] Read aloud: does it sound like the user talking to a friend, or like written prose?

## Output format

1. **Content type** and why
2. **3 hook options** (labelled by type), with a recommendation
3. **The script** in two columns — spoken line | on-screen text/visual — with second markers, and the four spine beats marked
4. **Word count, estimated runtime, and the sentence-length map** — the per-line word counts in a row (`9 · 11 · 8 · 16 · 15`), so the rhythm is visible rather than assumed
5. **The caption/CTA**

One script, one cut — the same video goes to Reels, TikTok and Shorts. Don't produce per-platform
variants unless the user asks for them.

Write the script as a **road map, not a word-for-word recital** unless asked. Word-for-word
reads robotic on camera; a one-page map with locked-in facts lets the natural flow survive.
When delivering a verbatim draft, add: "read this twice, then say it in your own words."

## Working with AI on scripts

Use AI for **research, outlines, fact-checking and roasting a draft — not the finished script**.
AI prose is generic and tone-deaf to the creator's voice. Sequence: Ideas → Outline → Fact check
→ draft in the creator's tone with their past scripts as reference. Cross-check every stated
fact; confidently wrong numbers kill credibility faster than a weak hook.
