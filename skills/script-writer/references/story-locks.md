# Story Locks & Sentence-Level Craft

Story Locks (Kallaway) are word patterns that lock attention *after* the hook. Contrast is
the bedrock — the other five are all contrast wearing different clothes.

---

## 1. Term Branding
Give a concept a name instead of just explaining it. Triggers the labeling effect: a named
thing feels important, and the viewer can't leave until they hear the definition.

Examples in the wild: "Value Equation" (Hormozi), "1,000 True Fans" (Kevin Kelly),
"Lego Brick Framework", "Story Locks" itself.

**Apply:** before publishing, ask "can I package this idea and name it?" Reserve it for your
contrarian takes and best frameworks — don't name everything.

## 2. Embedded Truths
Frame the claim as already true rather than as an option. Every hedge word is a micro-doubt —
a tiny exit door attention walks through.

| Weak | Embedded |
|---|---|
| "**If** you try this…" | "**When** you try this…" |
| "This might work because…" | "The reason this works so well…" |
| "You might notice this…" | "Once you see it, you can't unsee it…" |
| "Some creators might be making a mistake…" | "The mistake most creators make is…" |

## 3. Thought Narration
Say out loud what the viewer is already thinking. Builds instant trust ("they must know what
they're talking about") and turns a one-to-many video into a simulated one-to-one conversation —
personalisation at scale.

- "Now you're sitting there thinking…"
- "If I were you, the biggest thing on my mind right now would be…"
- "Now the question you're probably thinking is…"
- "If there's one thing you're worried about, it's probably…"

**Apply:** after every major point, ask "what should the viewer be thinking right now?" — then
say it out loud at the transition. A few times per video, not constantly.

## 4. Negative Frames
Loss aversion: ~2× more motivated to avoid pain than to gain a reward. Convert advice into
a warning.

- "Here's how to build a strong personal brand" → "The worst possible thing you could do for your personal brand is this."
- Triple-negative intensifier: "Never ever ever…"
- Soft negative: "You're making content way harder than it has to be."

**Negative framing is not negative phrasing.** This lock is about pointing at a loss the viewer
wants to avoid — the sentence itself still states something as true. It does not license
stacked negatives ("no jailbreak, no workaround") or was-never reversals ("the problem was never
X"), which are on the delete list below. Frame the stakes as loss; write the sentence as a claim.

## 5. Loop Openers (re-hooking)
Every viewer's attention is an hourglass. The hook flips it; after **20–30 seconds** of straight
delivery the sand runs out and they churn — that's the decaying retention graph. A loop opener
flips the hourglass again by confirming the current value and teasing bigger value next.

Cadence: **every 20–30s for short-form**, every 60–90s for long-form.

- "Here's the thing though…"
- "But that's not even the most interesting part…"
- "This is where it gets crazy…"
- "Most people stop here, but actually…"
- "Here's what nobody talks about…"
- "That was powerful, but the next one is even more important."

Note every one contains a contrast word. Not a coincidence.

## 6. Contrast Words
Curiosity is a gap between what the viewer expects and what you claim is true. Contrast words
build the bridge across that gap.

**but** (strongest) · **actually** (what you thought was wrong) · **instead** (redirect) ·
**turns out** (revelation) · **except** (exception to an assumption) · **yet** (contradiction)

- "Actually, the data shows the opposite."
- "Instead of posting this, try this."
- "It turns out the algorithm doesn't care about that at all."
- "Every creator does this — except the ones that are actually growing."

## Sentence-level craft

**South Park rule.** Linear is boring: "this happens, and then this happens."
Write: "this happens, **but** this happens, **therefore** this happens."

**Rhythm.** Beginners write every sentence the same length — monotone, and the ear checks out.
Vary it: a short one, then a longer one that carries the idea through a turn, then short again.
Short still means a complete sentence. "It cost them nothing" is short; "Cost them nothing" is a
fragment, and fragments sound like a script being read rather than a person talking.

**Specificity.** Anchor with hard facts you'd otherwise forget on camera: dates, names, numbers
("2021", "19 years old", "$1.4 billion", "30 minutes down to 10"). Write these down separately
from the flow — they're the part memory drops under a lens.

**"You" not "I".** Nobody cares what mistake you made. Aim it at the viewer.

**Simplicity.** Plain vocabulary, no jargon — never in the first 30 seconds. If a technical
term is unavoidable, define it on the spot.

**Spoken, not written.** Read every paragraph aloud. If you stumble, or it doesn't sound like
you, rewrite it. Don't fake a word or a style that isn't yours.

## The delete list

Cut on sight:

- **Hedges:** if · maybe · might · could · probably — replace with *when*, *the reason*, *once you do this*, *this is how it works*
- **Greetings:** "Hi, my name is…" · "Welcome to my channel/blog…" · "In today's video we will learn…"
- **Personal preamble:** what you ate, where you're filming, what you're doing later
- **Vague generalities:** "most people make mistakes" → name the exact mistake and who's making it
- **Filler and slow delivery** in the first 3 seconds
- **Generic goal words** as a hook ("grow on YouTube")
- **Stacked negatives:** "no jailbreak, no workaround, no trick" → state the positive fact: "They just did it"
- **Was-never reversals:** "the problem was never X, it's Y" → drop the first half, keep the claim
- **Two-part open loops:** "but X isn't the story, Y is" → say the Y half alone, in one short line
- **Empty pointer phrases:** "this is the one" · "here's the thing" · "and this is important" — they announce significance instead of delivering it
- **Codenames and version numbers** in spoken lines → describe what the thing now does; the name goes in the caption
- **Clipped fragments:** "First time in two years." · "Same chips, more model." → restore the subject and verb: "That's the first time in two years."
- **Rhythmic triples:** "every data centre, every model, every lab" · "no setup, no config, no code" — the strongest tell of AI-written copy. Replace with one concrete example
- **Synonym rotation:** the same thing called *holes*, then *flaws*, then *vulnerabilities* across three beats — pick one and repeat it
- **A third conjunction opener:** two lines starting on "And"/"So" is spoken rhythm, four is an expanded outline
- **Explainer lead-ins:** "You see" · "Here's the thing" · "The truth is" · "What you need to understand is" — narrator phrases that announce an explanation instead of giving one. If a line needs one to connect, reorder the line so it carries the previous sentence's subject
- **The outro:** "bye", "like and subscribe", the wave

## Two process tests

**The friend test.** Explain the topic to 4–5 friends before writing. Each retelling finds a
more natural, less linear pattern — that flow *is* your script. Note it down, add the hard
facts, and you have your road map.

**Read-aloud test.** Rehearse the map 2–3 times on camera before the real take. The brain
rebuilds the conversational flow by take three; word-for-word memorisation never gets there.
